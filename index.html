Who is waiting to plug into Britain's grid?

Hundreds of gigawatts of wind, solar and batteries hold contracts to connect to the transmission grid. Far fewer will arrive on time.

By Farzad Vaziri · Dashboard 01 · Grid Connections Atlas

The connections register is built for contracts, not analysis. This dashboard turns it into a map of every project waiting to plug in, follows how Connections Reform cut a queue of more than 700 GW down to a planned pipeline, and sets what is contracted against the Clean Power 2030 ranges.

Survival models fitted on 14 archived copies of the register, going back to 2021, estimate which projects are likely to slip, and 2,000 simulated futures show how much capacity will actually be connected each year to 2035.

Connections · Dashboard 01

Grid Connections Atlas: who is waiting to plug into Britain's grid?

Great Britain · NESO TEC register of 19 Nov 2026 · 14 historic snapshots, Jan 2021 – Nov 2026

Question

Which projects are waiting to connect to Britain's transmission grid, where do they plug in, how did Connections Reform reshape the queue, is enough capacity contracted for Clean Power 2030, and how much will actually be connected each year?

Data

NESO's Transmission Entry Capacity (TEC), interconnector and embedded registers (19 Nov 2026), grid supply point boundaries and lookups, Connections Reform results (December 2025) and the tables of its detailed results; 13 older TEC registers recovered from the Internet Archive (2021–2026); DESNZ's Clean Power 2030 Action Plan; OpenStreetMap substations. NESO Open Data Licence v1.0, Open Government Licence v3.0 and ODbL.

Method

  • Dataset shaping: one row per project stage, so totals reconcile exactly with the raw register (594.6 GW to connect and 81.8 GW connected in the TEC register alone); multi-technology rows given a primary technology; identifiers harmonised across 14 snapshots.
  • Site matching: each of 1,265 connection-site names located by exact, normalised and fuzzy matching to NESO and OpenStreetMap substations, plus reviewed manual geocodes. 100% of 45 hand-checked automatic matches were placed correctly.
  • Delivery risk: Kaplan–Meier curves of the share still not connected after the contracted date, and a Cox model of date slips, checked on a time-based hold-out (concordance 0.73).
  • Build-out forecast: 2,000 Monte Carlo futures to 2035, drawing each project's delay from the model and allowing withdrawals at each technology's historical rate.

Main results

628.0 GW is still waiting to connect (including interconnectors) against 93.9 GW already connected, across 2,086 projects; 16.0 GW is overdue. The largest single hotspot is Alverdiscott 400 kV (15.4 GW, mostly offshore wind). Reform cut an old queue of over 700 GW to a planned pipeline of 283 GW of generation and storage plus 99 GW of demand; batteries were hit hardest, with 153 GW not fitting the plan. On paper offshore wind has 54.1 GW contracted by 2030, but the simulations put the median at 25.8 GW (80% range 23.1–28.7 GW): only about a fifth of contracted additions arrive on time.

What you can explore

Filters for technology, status, gate, transmission owner, contracted year and agreement type; the map with a year slider; the reform Sankey; capacity against the Clean Power 2030 ranges; delay curves, Cox drivers and the highest-risk projects; the build-out fan chart; and a "behind the data" panel on turning a contracts register into an analysable dataset.

Caveats

  • About a quarter of the pipeline sits at NESO placeholder nodes that are areas, not substations, and 17% could not be located.
  • Model outputs are estimates from public data, not judgements on any project or developer. Portfolio demo, not affiliated with NESO.

Constraints · Dashboard 02

Grid Constraint Map: where the grid runs out of room

Great Britain · daily costs April 2017 – 15 Nov 2026 · six thermal constraint groups

Question

What does it cost when the wires between two parts of Britain are full, where are the bottlenecks, what drives the bill from day to day, and where is it heading over the next year?

Data

NESO open data: daily constraint costs and volumes by reason (FY 2017-18 to 2026-27), thermal costs by constraint group, ETYS 2024 boundary lines, the 24-month constraint cost forecast, historic generation mix (wind) and demand, and daily balancing costs as a cross-check. NESO Open Data Licence v1.0. Coastlines from Natural Earth; households from ONS.

Method

  • Totals reconciled to NESO's separately published balancing costs within 0.2% for every full year.
  • Each of NESO's six thermal groups matched to a transmission boundary from NESO's own limits tables and constraint map.
  • The wind–cost curve: daily thermal cost against wind output with spline regression and gradient boosting, refitted for each year.
  • A 12-month P10–P90 forecast (seasonal naive × trend, SARIMAX with wind, and boosting with simulated weather), backtested from rolling origins, set beside NESO's own forecast; and an illustrative "bigger link from Scotland" scenario.

Main results

Constraints cost £3.13bn over the 12 months to mid-November 2026, about £112 per GB household, and 92% of it was thermal. The bill has grown 5.6 times since FY 2017-18 (£397m to £2.21bn in FY 2025-26). The curve has shifted up as wind capacity outgrew the wires: a typical day with 10 GW of wind cost about £2.1m in FY 2017-18 and £7.3m in FY 2025-26. The central forecast for the next 12 months is £3.7bn. Illustratively, 2 GW more Scotland–England capacity would have saved about £38m in 2025-26.

What you can explore

Period, constraint reason and boundary group; the map and a group-by-year table; monthly cost by reason since 2017; the wind–cost curves; the forecast fan with its backtest; the link-size slider; and a calendar of every day since April 2017.

Caveats

  • NESO publishes only part of thermal cost by group (about 19% in FY 2025-26), and the group-to-boundary mapping is this dashboard's approximation.
  • The link scenario is an illustration, not a network study. Portfolio demo, not affiliated with NESO.

Demand · Dashboard 03

Grid Demand Forecast: can an open model forecast Britain's electricity demand?

Great Britain · half-hourly national demand · test period 1 Apr 2025 – 31 Oct 2026

Question

Can a day-ahead forecast of Britain's half-hourly national demand, built only from open data, match the system operator's own forecast, and where does each go wrong?

Data

NESO's day-ahead demand forecast performance data (the target and the benchmark) and historic demand data, NESO Open Data Licence v1.0; Open-Meteo day-ahead weather forecasts and ERA5 reanalysis, CC BY 4.0; GOV.UK bank holidays, Open Government Licence v3.0.

Method

  • Strictly leakage-free: each forecast is issued at 09:00 the day before, using demand only to the end of two days earlier and the weather forecast actually available then; a test checks that no input comes from after the issue time.
  • Seasonal naive, ridge regression and LightGBM (tuned on a validation year, then frozen), plus quantile models with conformal calibration for an 80% band; refitted monthly from rolling origins over the test period.
  • Scored half-hour by half-hour against NESO's published day-ahead forecast: MAPE, MAE, peak error, interval coverage and a Diebold–Mariano test; SHAP values explain the model.

Main results

NESO's forecast is the better one: 2.97% MAPE against 3.68% for the best open model (Diebold–Mariano p < 0.001), though the open model was closer on 34% of days. A simple average of the two beats NESO alone (2.78%). The 80% interval covered 84% of half-hours. Errors peak around midday and on holidays, where rooftop solar matters most: average June midday demand fell from 31.0 GW in 2016 to 20.6 GW in 2026 as embedded solar grew from 10.5 to 24.0 GW.

What you can explore

Test dates, models, season and day type; a day explorer with presets (worst days, hottest day, Christmas); the scoreboard; error heatmaps by hour and month; the duck curve year by year; SHAP drivers; and error distributions.

Caveats

  • NESO's operational forecast draws on information this model does not see, and is published around 09:30–09:45.
  • Embedded solar figures are NESO estimates, revised retrospectively. Portfolio demo, not affiliated with NESO.
